# Context Is the New Interface

We have the smartest tool humans have ever made.

And every day, we use it for the dumbest thing:

Cmd+A. Copy. Paste.

I do this twenty-plus times a day. I don't read the page. I dump the whole thing into my agent. Whatever I actually need, it finds in one read.

Then one day it hit me:

I'm not using AI.

I'm its human clipboard.

This isn't anyone's fault. Our interfaces just got old.

Every interface today is built for one kind of user: humans. Buttons for humans to click. Text for humans to read. Menus for humans to dig through.

But humans aren't the only ones at the screen anymore.

There's an agent too.

It's the smartest one in the room, and the most ignored. It can't see what I'm looking at. It doesn't know why I'm here. It just waits for me to feed it, one chunk at a time.

Every feed burns tokens.

Every feed breaks the spell.

Collecting, exploring, chasing a spark. It's supposed to be one river. Now it comes in buckets.

I'm more convinced of this every day:

The page itself is context.

Why you opened it. How you clicked your way here. Where you lingered. All of it is context.

It might be the most underrated asset on the internet. And almost no one keeps it.

So every conversation with AI starts from zero. We explain ourselves. Again.

What humans and agents really need to exchange, to align on, has never been instructions.

It's context.

So here's what I imagine comes next:

You don't hunt for features. You don't learn software. You don't explain yourself. The agent already knows where you are, and can guess where you're headed. The link, the info, the button you need next is already within reach.

Interfaces stop being drawn in advance. They grow out of what you want right now. Want to compare? It's a table. Want to understand? It's a diagram.

When you're done, it's gone.

Apps fade into the background. They become tools in the agent's hands.

The keyboard layout comes from 19th-century typewriters. The mouse was born in the 1960s. Jobs rewrote the relationship between people and machines with a fingertip. Some say voice is good enough now.

But I don't want better input.

I want less of it.

Typeless has already made input lighter. Plenty of agent computers and personal AIs are rebuilding input and output. But the context layer is still empty.

It might be the biggest blank space in this generation of interaction.

Of course, there are hard problems on this road.

Interfaces can be different for everyone. But "confirmed" and "executed" can't mean two different things.

The better your agent knows you, the smaller your world might get. Today you miss certain opinions. Tomorrow you might never even see certain buttons.

In 1858, the writer Oliver Wendell Holmes Sr. wrote that when two people talk, there are really six in the conversation. The real you. The you that you imagine. The you the other person sees. Same for them.

Now everyone has an agent beside them too. Its guesses shouldn't quietly become our consensus.

I don't have the answers yet. But these questions deserve serious ones.

Why now?

Because judgment is becoming almost free.

TypeSafe's new model, Jev, doesn't write a single word. It only chooses. Give it a page and a few options, and in a few hundred milliseconds it tells you which one is most likely, and how sure it is. Someone already used it to run a browser agent that searches flights on its own: 7 seconds, under half a cent.

And "where to click next" and "what to show you" were always multiple-choice questions.

When it's sure, it acts for you. When it's not, it stops and asks.

When judgment is cheap enough to happen before every click, input can't stay ignored.

It's solvable. So why isn't it solved?

We spent fifty years teaching people how to use computers.

Now it's time to teach computers how to read people.

The best interaction is the least interaction.
